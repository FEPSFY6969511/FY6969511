Abstract

    Coastal sea walls are vital in protecting infrastructure and communities from erosion, flooding and wave damage. 
    This article assesses how structural geometry, foundation stability and the very material influence the function of sea walls. 
    The article identifies crucial challenges faced such as: scour, physical deterioration, overtopping and wave loading.
    Furthermore, research from Bal Yetim et al. (2026) assesses the impact of concrete panels reinforced with (GFRP) bars. 
    Whilst, Dong et al. (2026) examines how ridge dimensions and crest elevation affect wave overtopping. 
    Overall, this article concludes that affected sea defences like sea walls are effective under the terms that they require
    a structural and geotechnical design, integrated hydraulics, sustained by consistent maintenance. In addition to, 
    preparation and innovation for the escalating epidemic of future sea-level rise.

Introduction

    As a result, of rising sea levels coastal erosion and flooding threaten coastal land and surge pressure on existing defences.
    the main objective of sea walls  are to absorb/deflect wave action and protect infrastructure. Conversely, their effectiveness
    are contingent on more than position on the shore and height. Factors such as: material property, structural foundation and local 
    wave action impair their performance. This article explores how engineering factors affect coastal protection, presenting experimental
    research and utilising Dawlish sea wall as a case study.

Design, materials and foundations 

    The geometry of sea walls determine how the incoming wave energy is managed. Vertical sea walls provide compact protection but reflect
    extensive energy, whilst sloped sea walls support wave run-up and dissipation. As a result, recurved structures redirect water seawards
    which could reduce overtopping.

    Reinforced concrete is commonly used due to its compressive strength and tensile reinforcement Bal Yetim et al. (2026) investigated concrete 
    seawall panels reinforced with glass-fibre-reinforced polymer (GFRP) bars. Their experiments compared compressive strengths of 50, 60 and 75 MPa 
    and reinforcement ratios of 0.33%, 0.50% and 0.83%. Higher reinforcement ratios improved impact response, whereas increasing concrete strength 
    could increase brittleness, particularly above 60 MPa. These findings demonstrate that material strength must be balanced with impact resistance.
    Foundation stability is equally important. Wave forces may cause sliding or overturning, requiring sufficient structural resistance and suitable
    ground conditions. Scour can also remove sediment around the seawall toe, weakening foundation support. Buried foundations, rock armour and toe 
    protection help reduce this risk (Environment Agency, 2012).
    Effective design therefore combines hydraulic performance, material durability and geotechnical stability.

Case study: Dawlish seawall, Devon

    The Dawlish seawall highlights the significance of sea defence engineering in preserving vital infrastructure. In February 2014, railway
    foundations and the seawall were severely damaged due to the storm. Consequently, closing the railway connection for approximately 8 weeks
    (Network Rail, 2024). This led to redevelopment of the seawall, through the Southwest rail resilience programme. The seawall was enhanced by 
    incorporating recurved sections and raising the height to 8 metres. These enhancements aimed to reduce overtopping and to better protect
    surrounding infrastructure e.g. the railway. This reflects the significance of structural geometry identified by Dong et al. (2026).
    The redesigned established an effective  performance during Storm Ciarán in 2023. However, is it is relatively new consistent monitoring is 
    required to assess the integrity of the foundation and long term impacts from future severe wave action.

Limitations and Improvements

    Seawalls have both protective benefits, but structural and environmental limitations. For example, repeated impacts from high wave energy
    can weaken and deteriorate the foundations as wells as increase scour. Bal Yetim et al. (2026) demonstrated that higher concrete strength 
    does not necessarily improve impact resistance, emphasising the importance of reinforcement design. Consequences of the climbing epidemic 
    of climate change has resulted in higher wave energy, increasing overtopping frequency. Therefore, to mitigate this problem higher crests, 
    stronger foundations and recurved parapets have to be implemented. Methods for assessing overtopping have been provided by EurOtop (2018). 
    As a result, frequent inspections and maintenance are vital for long-term performance.

Conclusuion

    Overall, this article highlights that coastal seawall functioning depends on wave energy/action, material and stability of foundation.
    Furthermore, the Dawlish case study demonstrates the significance of adapting the defence following rising sea levels and more intense
    meteorological events. Experimental research highlights how berm dimensions affect overtopping and impact resistance. Ultimately, efficient
    sea defence engineering, requires consideration of future coastal conditions, regular maintenance, integrated hydraulics and a sustainable  
    design.
